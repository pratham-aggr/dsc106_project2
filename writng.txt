
DESIGN RATIONALE BULLETS FOR VIZ1
Using Substantiated "Rates" rather than Raw Counts: 2.0
Rather than using the raw data , we used percentages in order to avoid overwhelming readers. It would also violate readability and effectiveness of the graph when there are too many data points covering what we are trying to convey. So by using volume to convey our percentage rates, we are able to convey the message that Black New Yorkers make up a disproportionate number of complaints for the NYPD. This additionally ensures that data is normalized by each group’s total population, which helps show a pattern that may be hidden if a population had a far lower total population than others.

Bar Graph with Statistical Highlights: 1.5
We decided to also use a bar chart because it would make sense logically for readers to interpret every racial group to be proportional. We include a helpful line indicating where a complaint rate proportional to the given population would be. We use a bar graph that quickly conveys the difference in reporting rates between our populations. This also allows us to sort our  graph highest to lowest to make comparison even easier for the lay person.

Maintaining a Zero-Baseline: 2.0
Having all of our bar charts start out from 0% makes it easily interpretable for readers as they are given the task of doing any form of mental math or calculation t o understand the graph. It also helps prevent the deceptive practice of axis-truncation as it keeps the values clear to the readers.

Dual-Axis Combo Chart (Line over Bars): -1.5

While we did acknowledge the staggering number of complaints coming from Black individuals as to not violate lying by omission, it gets visually overridden by the giant stable rate line. Readers are drawn to the unchanging line which emphasizes their takeaway that the number of actually substantiated complaints being relatively equal. Forcing the two visuals together along with the title really pushes for the message that there does not appear to be a bias against Black New Yorkers. 

Focusing on Total Number of Complaints Over Severity
Additionally, we focus on the number of complaints made by each group rather than the severity of complaints. This means we do not show whether or not any particular group is more likely to be targeted by more serious harassment, and reports as such in their complaints.This is a serious breach ethically as there is a large difference in severity between rude language and sexual misconduct,or excessive use of force. As this visualization is downplaying any potential biases, this would be an important piece of information to help readers get an accurate picture of what each group is filing complaints over.

Specific Ordering of the X-Axis By Descending Order : -1.0
We made the choice of sorting the number of substantiated complaints by descending order. This  would place complaints made by Asian New Yorkers to be at the highest. This matches with our narrative that “Asian and White individuals actually have higher complaint substantiation rates than Black individuals.” Emphasizing the order visually really reinforces that there is truth in the claim that we are making and readers are more easily persuaded into believing the title’s claim. 
Color Palette for Stacked Bars: 1.0
We decided to use distinctive colors to label the different types of claims with green being “Substantiated” and a background grey representing “Unsubstantiated.” This allows readers to more easily identify the types of claims being made and it is a logical and readable choice to make to convey information to readers. There is also consistency throughout each racial group’s bar. It also visually reinforces the claim the title is making for Black individuals. 

FINAL REFLECTION

We created these visualizations supporting our claim that the New York Police Department likely does have some systemic racial bias against Black New Yorkers as demonstrated in the overwhelming number of complaints belonging to Black individuals. Visually, not only does it look better, we feel it is more straightforward for readers to understand and to interpret the message being presented. At a quick glance and reading of each visualization's title, we are able to easily convey our message to readers. It also utilizes way fewer deceptive practices as it intends to convey the obvious truth being demonstrated in the data without it being misused so that it gets misconstrued and reinterpreted. In this way, we are giving readers as honest of a truth as we can without having to use methods to construct a different or twisted message other than what we are given in the dataset.

This project opened our eyes to both how we can adjust what we focus on to influence the audience, and what we avoid focusing on. This project helped define "ethical analysis and visualization" further to mean that each part of forming a proposition, analysis, and the visualization must cohesively fit together in the simplest way possible. For example: our second visualization would not be as dishonest had we purely focused on the rate of complaint validation without extending our claims to policing as a whole. Because our claim critiques the validity of complaints by certain populations it influences viewers to see police complaints by Black and Hispanic citizens as less valid, which ignores any underlying structural issues. We must focus on what the data means and provide a holistic representation of it in an understandable format, as we have access to the whole dataset and viewers of our visualizations do not.

The second visualization's persuasive power comes almost entirely from its design decisions rather than from the data itself. By overlaying a nearly flat rate line on top of stacked bars, we exploit a known perceptual bias: readers' eyes follow the line, and its horizontal stability communicates "no difference" before the bar heights — which show Black complainants filing complaints at nearly triple the volume of the next largest group — are even registered. Sorting the x-axis by descending substantiation rate (Asian → White → Hispanic → Black) further anchors the reader in the high-rate groups before they reach Black complainants, reinforcing the title's claim from the moment they begin reading left to right. Most critically, presenting only complaint counts by group ignores the severity of what was alleged: if Black and Hispanic complainants disproportionately filed complaints about excessive force or sexual misconduct rather than discourtesy, a slightly lower raw substantiation rate could still reflect serious misconduct rather than equal treatment. That omission is what makes the second visualization's "Against" argument structurally dishonest even though every individual number it shows is accurate.

A misleading design choice is one that falsely claims to describe more of the data than it actually does. Had we compared substantiated complaints by proportion of the population, rather than just the proportion of substantiated complaints, we would still see outsized targeting of Black and Hispanic communities and it would remove room for doubt on whether the complaints were legitimate. This does introduce difficulty when we must simplify our findings into easy to understand visualizations, as it can be difficult to find the most representative statistics to use. Similarly, design choices such as non-linear axes distort the impact our statistic may have, to strengthen the claim. The limits of persuasive and dishonest design lies in whether we are enhancing an existing trend in the data, or overwriting one. We already manipulate the data to show trends, and there are valid use cases for log-transformations and similar manipulations, so long as we are open with our readers about what we have done and why we have done so.
